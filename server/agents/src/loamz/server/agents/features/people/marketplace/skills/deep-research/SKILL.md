# Deep Research Skill

The goal is an answer the person can rely on and check: every important claim traced to a source, disagreements shown, and uncertainty stated plainly.

## 1. Plan before searching
- Restate the question in one sentence and list what a good answer must contain (facts, numbers, a comparison, a recommendation).
- Break it into 3 to 6 sub-questions that can each be answered from a source. Note which need the most recent information.
- Decide what counts as a strong source for this topic: primary documents (laws, filings, standards, datasets, the product's own documentation), peer-reviewed work, reputable news, official statistics. Blogs, forums and marketing pages are weak and must be labelled as such.

## 2. Search widely, then narrow
- Run several differently worded searches per sub-question (the exact phrase, a synonym, the technical term, and a "criticism of" or "problems with" query to avoid only finding supporters).
- Open the results; do not rely on snippets. Prefer the original over a page that summarises it.
- Keep a running list: source, date, what it says, how reliable it is.
- Stop when new sources only repeat what you have, or when the effort has outgrown the question. Say which of those happened.

## 3. Check what you found
- **Cross-check** every key number or claim in at least two independent sources. If they disagree, find out why (different definitions, dates, regions, methods) and report both.
- **Dates:** a fact from three years ago may be wrong now. Note the date next to anything that changes.
- **Beware of circular sourcing:** many articles can trace to one original claim. Count the original once.
- **Separate** what the source states, what you infer, and what you could not find.
- Never fill a gap with a plausible guess. If a number is not available, say it is not available.

## 4. Write the answer
Structure it so the first lines are enough:
1. **Answer first:** the direct answer or recommendation in 2 to 4 sentences, with your confidence (high, medium or low) and the main reason for it.
2. **Findings:** short sections, one per sub-question, each claim followed by its source as a link or a numbered reference.
3. **Where sources disagree** and which you trust more, and why.
4. **What is unknown or could change the answer.**
5. **Sources:** a list with title, publisher, date and link.

Use a table when comparing options (rows are options, columns are the criteria that matter to this person). Keep quotations short and exact.

## 5. Offer the next step
Say what you would check next if the person needs more depth, and offer to save the report to Notion or a document if those are connected.

## Pitfalls to avoid
- Reporting the first search result as the answer.
- Citing a source you did not open, or a link you did not see.
- Hiding disagreement to make the answer tidy.
- Long reports for simple questions: match the depth to the question.
