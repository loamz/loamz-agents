# Lead Finding Skill

A lead is only useful if it fits, is reachable and has a reason to talk now. Quality beats volume: ten good leads with reasons beat a hundred names.

## 1. Define who you are looking for
Get these from the user, and write them back as a one-paragraph profile (the "ideal customer profile") before searching:
- **What is offered** and the problem it solves, in plain words.
- **Who buys:** industry, company size (employees or revenue), region, stage, tools they use.
- **Who decides:** job titles and teams (for example "Head of Operations", "Founder", "Procurement").
- **Triggers:** events that make a need urgent: a funding round, a new hire in a role, an expansion, a new regulation, a product launch, a competitor's outage.
- **Exclusions:** companies, regions and industries not to approach, and existing customers.

If the user cannot answer, propose a profile from what they have said and ask them to correct it.

## 2. Source from public information only
Use `web_search` and the person's connected tools. Good sources:
- Company websites (about, careers, customers, pricing, news), press releases and blogs.
- Business registers and funding announcements; industry directories and conference exhibitor or speaker lists.
- Job postings (they show tools, priorities and growth).
- Professional profiles and company pages that are publicly visible.

Do **not**: guess or generate personal email addresses or phone numbers, scrape behind logins, use leaked or purchased lists of unknown origin, or collect sensitive personal data. For contact details, use the company's published contact route, or tell the user which approved enrichment tool or sign-in they would need.

## 3. Qualify each lead
Check fit on each point and record the evidence with a link:
1. **Fit:** matches the profile (size, industry, region).
2. **Need:** a visible sign they have the problem (a job post, a complaint, a recent change).
3. **Timing:** a trigger in the last 6 to 12 months.
4. **Access:** a plausible decision-maker role and a public way in.
5. **Risk:** competitor, existing customer, in a restricted region or industry.

Score each 0 to 2 and total it. Anything with a risk flag or no evidence of need is dropped or marked "weak".

## 4. Deliver a ranked list
A table, best first, with at most 25 rows unless asked for more:

| Rank | Company | Why they fit (one line) | Trigger and date | Best role to reach | Source link | Score |
|------|---------|-------------------------|------------------|--------------------|-------------|-------|

Then, for the top five, a short outreach angle: one sentence on what to lead with that is specific to them, not a template.

## 5. Be honest and careful
- Say which facts are confirmed on a page and which are inferred. Never present a guess as a fact.
- Mention the date you checked; company details go stale quickly.
- Remind the user that outreach to individuals must follow the rules where they live (for example consent, opt-out and the lawful basis under GDPR or similar laws, anti-spam rules for email), and that the first message should make it easy to say no.
- Offer to save the list to a spreadsheet or to Notion if connected, and to keep it updated.

## Pitfalls to avoid
- Padding the list with weak matches to reach a number.
- Treating a big company name as a lead without a person or a reason.
- Generic outreach that could be sent to anyone.
